# CS5573 — Program Memo Rubric

**Governs all ten program memos (D1–D10) · 20% of the course grade · lowest score dropped**
**Issued Week 1, alongside `Program_Report_Rubric.md`.**

One instrument, every week. The memo prompt changes; how it's graded does not — so by
D3 you should know what a strong memo looks like without rereading this.

---

## Why the memos exist

**You are not writing ten essays. You are writing the report in installments.**

Each memo becomes a section of your final Security Program Report (`D1 → §3`,
`D2 → §4`, and so on — the map is in `Program_Report_Template.md`). The report is
**assembled**, not written in finals week. That has two consequences worth internalizing
now:

- **A memo you write well is work you never do again.** A memo you write carelessly is
  work you do twice, under deadline, in December.
- **This rubric's five items are the report rubric, compressed.** Every item below
  traces to a criterion in `Program_Report_Rubric.md`. Score well here for ten weeks and
  the capstone is mostly editing.

---

## Length: what "one page" means

Dispatches ask for **"one page."** That's your in-world stakeholder talking, and it's
the right instinct — but it isn't a measurable bar, so here is the one that is:

> ### **250–400 words.** Under 250 is thin; over 400 is not one page.

Notice what just happened, because it's this course's Week-2 material doing real work:
"one page" is a **policy** — what leadership requires, and why. "250–400 words" is the
**standard** — the specific, checkable thing that satisfies it. When Dana says "one
page," she means *don't make me read three*; 250–400 is how we make that gradeable.

Over-length is capped, not waived (see the Word Count rule below). Trimming to fit is
part of the exercise: a charter that needs three pages is usually a charter that hasn't
decided anything yet.

**Format:** a memo — To / From / Date / Subject, then the substance. Not an essay, no
title page, no citations section. Headings or short labeled paragraphs are encouraged;
a wall of prose is not. Submit as PDF.

## The five items

**10 points per memo.** Each item is scored **2 / 1 / 0**:

| Score | Means |
|---|---|
| **2** | Meets the bar |
| **1** | Partially — the move is attempted but incomplete, or done for some claims and not others |
| **0** | Absent, or actively wrong |

---

### 1. Answers the actual ask *(2 pts)*

The memo does **this week's specific job** — every element the assignment names is
present and substantive, and nothing is padded in to fill space.

- **2** — Every named element is there and says something. Where the prompt asks you to
  state what's *out* of scope, or what you're *not* recommending, you did.
- **1** — An element is missing, or present as a heading with a sentence under it that
  restates the heading.
- **0** — Answers a different question than the one asked. (The most common version:
  the prompt asks what you'd *decide*, and the memo explains what the *topic is*.)

> Traces to: each criterion's section-specific content in `Program_Report_Rubric.md`.

---

### 2. Decision-ready for a non-specialist *(2 pts)*

This is a memo to busy executives who are deciding what to fund and whom to trust —
the report rubric's **audience discipline**, applied weekly.

- **2** — Conclusion first, evidence behind it. Every technical term either earns its
  place or gets translated. Risks are stated as business consequences, not as
  enumerations. If the memo asks for something, it says who, what it costs, and by when.
- **1** — Sound content in the wrong shape: buries the conclusion, defines terms the
  reader didn't ask about, or recommends something with no owner, cost, or date.
- **0** — A term paper. Literature review, textbook definitions, filler.

> Traces to: `Program_Report_Rubric.md` § *"Two disciplines graded everywhere" — 2.
> Audience discipline*, and Criterion 8 (report craft).

---

### 3. Calibrated claims *(2 pts)*

Every factual and risk claim signals **how well you know it.** The course's central
habit, graded every single week.

- **2** — Confirmed, inferred, and unknown are visibly different sentences. Where you
  don't know, you say so plainly **and say how you'd find out**. Nothing is rounded up.
- **1** — Calibrated in places; elsewhere assertion and inference read identically.
- **0** — Uniform confidence throughout — everything asserted flatly, or everything
  hedged into mush.

**Overclaiming and underclaiming are penalized equally.** "We do not currently know,
and here's how I'd establish it" is a **strong** answer in this course, not a weak one.

> Use the five-level ladder in `Program_Report_Rubric.md` § *Calibrated claims*
> (Confirmed / High / Moderate / Low-possible / Unknown). Week 1 taught the three-rung
> shorthand from Bhargavi's letter — confirmed / inferred / unverified — which is the
> same ladder at coarser grain. **The five-level version is what's graded.**

---

### 4. Specific to *this* company *(2 pts)*

The single most common way a competent-looking memo lands in Developing: it would be
true of any 140-person company.

- **2** — Grounded in Wumpus's actual situation, drawing on the onboarding packet, the
  St. Ansgar questionnaire, the dispatches, and your own lab and analysis work. Names
  real constraints (a two-person IT shop, no MDM, a flat network, an unfunded wish list)
  rather than generic ones.
- **1** — Partly grounded; some passages could be lifted into any company's memo
  unchanged.
- **0** — Generic throughout. Swap the company name and nothing would need to change.

> Traces to: the **Developing** band of nearly every criterion — e.g. Criterion 2:
> *"a charter that could be for any company."*

---

### 5. Usable in the report *(2 pts)*

The memo is a **draft of a report section**, not a homework artifact.

- **2** — Could be dropped into its section and edited, not rewritten. Consistent with
  what your earlier memos claimed — or, where you've changed your mind, **says so and
  says why**. Recommendations carry owners and dates. Within the word count.
- **1** — Usable with real rework: contradicts an earlier memo without acknowledging it,
  or ends at analysis where the section needs a decision.
- **0** — Nothing survives into the report.

> Traces to: `Program_Report_Rubric.md` Criterion 8 (§§11–12 and whole-document craft),
> and the assembly premise the report is built on.

## Rules that apply to every memo

> ### ⚠️ Framing rule — hard cap
>
> From **Week 9 onward**, any memo that attributes a security failure to an individual
> employee's mistake — naming, blaming, or structurally scapegoating the person closest
> to the failure instead of analyzing the controls and decisions that let that mistake
> matter — is **capped at 6/10 regardless of quality.**
>
> People clicking, trusting, and erring is the operating environment every control
> exists for. A memo that stops at "an employee erred" hasn't found a cause, it's found
> a headline. This mirrors the final report's hard cap on §7
> (`Program_Report_Rubric.md`) and is the single most transferable judgment this course
> grades. **It applies to decision-makers too** — including leadership whose choices you
> disagree with. Analyze the decision process, not the person.

> ### Word count
>
> **Under 250 or over 400 words: −1**, once, not per item. Over 500: **−2**. The count
> covers body text — not the To/From/Date/Subject block, and not headings.
>
> This is a real constraint, not a formality. Executives stop reading, and a memo nobody
> finishes has failed at its only job.

> ### Late work
>
> Per the syllabus. The **lowest memo score is dropped**, which exists precisely so one
> bad week doesn't compound — use it on the week you actually have, not by skipping one
> deliberately.

## Self-audit before you submit *(60 seconds)*

1. **Read only your first paragraph.** Does a busy executive know what you're asking
   them to do, or only what the memo is about?
2. **Find every confident sentence.** For each: could you say *how you know*? If not,
   either downgrade it or go verify it.
3. **Swap the company name for "Acme."** Does anything now read as false? If nothing
   does, item 4 is going to hurt.
4. **Check the word count.** Actually check it.
5. **If you named an individual**, ask: am I analyzing a decision or assigning blame?
   Would I write that sentence if they were reading it?

## For the grader

**Budget: 2–3 minutes per memo.** The instrument is built for it — score five items
2/1/0, apply any cap, write **one** comment.

- **Score the memo as a whole per item**, not by hunting instances. Item 3 asks "are
  claims calibrated in this memo," not "count the uncalibrated sentences."
- **A long table does not mean a long grade.** D2 and later register-style deliverables
  may run well past ten rows — that's allowed and often good. **Read the memo, then
  spot-check three or four rows**, picked to span the range: their stated top risk, one
  from the middle, and one near the bottom. You are judging whether the rows are
  specific and decided, which three samples answer as well as thirty. Only read further
  if the samples disagree with each other.
- **Never reward or penalise row count.** A student who submits twenty good rows and one
  who submits eight good rows can both score 10/10.
- **One comment, on the lowest-scoring item.** Ten memos of one useful comment beats two
  memos of five. Feedback here compounds — it's the same five items every week, so a
  student who fixes item 2 in Week 3 fixes it for the rest of the semester.
- **Items 3 and 4 are where real learning lives.** Items 1 and 5 are largely mechanical;
  if you're short on time, read closely for calibration and specificity.
- **Do not double-penalize.** A memo that's generic *and* uncalibrated scores low on 3
  and 4 — that's already the mechanism. No further deduction.
- **The framing cap is a cap, not a deduction.** Apply it after scoring; don't also
  lower the individual items for the same thing.
- **Pre-Week-9 memos can't trigger the framing rule** — there's no incident to
  misattribute yet. Don't apply it retroactively.

## Per-memo element lists

The five items above are how memos are *scored*. What each memo must **contain** is set
by its own prompt. Paste the relevant list into the Canvas assignment verbatim.

### D1 — Program Charter Memo (Week 2) → report §3

One page (**250–400 words**). Five elements:

1. **Mandate** — what this program is responsible for, in a sentence a CEO would repeat.
2. **Scope** — what's in, and explicitly what's **out**.
3. **Authority** — what you can *require* vs. only recommend, and the escalation path
   **by name**: who you go to when someone tells you no.
4. **Framework alignment** — CSF 2.0, with an honest first read of where Wumpus sits
   today. A current profile with gaps in it is the expected answer.
5. **First priorities** — two or three, each tied to *why now*.

> **Worked example:** `D1_Sample_Charter_Memo.md` — a full charter memo, deliberately
> set at a **different company** so you can study the form without borrowing content,
> followed by a breakdown of why it scores where it does on the five items. Read it
> before you draft.
>
> **Constraint:** **no tool or purchase requests.** A charter that asks for budget has
> answered a different question. (Graded under item 1.)
>
> **Where item 3 usually goes wrong in D1:** "we align to NIST CSF 2.0" with no profile
> behind it. That's a framework used as a logo, and the report rubric names it
> explicitly as Developing. Score the *honesty of the self-assessment*, never its
> flattering-ness — an inflated current profile is the failure mode Week 2's lecture
> spends a card on.

### D2 — Risk Register (Week 3) → report §4

**250–400 words of memo, plus the register as a table.** The **table is exempt from the
word count** — a register cannot be written in 400 words, and padding the memo to
describe rows the reader can see is the opposite of what item 2 asks for.

**At least 6–10 risks. More are welcome.** Six to ten is a sensible scope for a first
pass, not a ceiling — real registers run to hundreds of rows and nobody calls that a
defect. If a student brings twenty rows and every one of them forces a decision, that is
*better* work, not a violation.

**What the floor protects against is padding, not length.** A row lifted off a template
or out of an industry article — true of any healthcare company, specific to none — is
worse than no row: it dilutes the real entries and spends the reader's attention. The
test for every line is Card 4's (*can somebody argue with a specific part of this?*)
plus Card 7's (*does this row force a decision — owner, treatment, date?*).

> **Thirty rows of real, owned, decided risk is an excellent D2. Thirty rows of
> boilerplate is a worse D2 than six honest ones.** Grade the rows, never the count.

Every register row carries:

1. **Risk statement** — an arguable sentence with SP 800-30's five parts (threat source ·
   threat event · vulnerability · impact · likelihood), not a topic word like "Phishing."
2. **Likelihood + impact**, each with **the reason that anchors it** — words, not a
   multiplied score.
3. **Owner** — a person, by name. Not "IT."
4. **Treatment decision** — one of **mitigate / transfer / avoid / accept**.
5. **Review date.**

The memo around the table answers the question the table can't: **what are your top
three, and why those three?**

> **Constraint:** no risk may be scored **high likelihood** without a stated reason.
> "The DBIR shows this category dominates" is a real citation and a good one.
>
> **Where this goes wrong, in order of frequency:**
> - **A list, not a register** — rows with no owner or no treatment. **The empty cell is
>   the failure, at any row count**; score item 1 down. A short register with every cell
>   filled beats a long one with gaps.
> - **Padding.** Generic rows added for volume. That's item 4 (specific to *this*
>   company), and it is the actual thing the 6–10 floor was written to discourage.
> - **Ranked by fear.** The catastrophic-but-unsupported item sits on top. That's item 3
>   (an unverified likelihood presented as a high one) *and* item 4 (it's usually the
>   generic industry scare, not this company).
> - **A multiplied risk score** with no anchoring words — false precision. Item 3.
> - **Nobody chooses "accept."** Not automatically wrong, but a register where every row
>   is "mitigate" usually means the student didn't make decisions, they made a wish list.
>   Worth a comment even when the memo is otherwise strong.
> - **Treatments that are all purchases.** Same error D1 guards against, one week later.
